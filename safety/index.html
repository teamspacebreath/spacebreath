Practise with care

Breathwork
safety guide.

Please read this before your first session, and come back to it before any practice with fast breathing or breath holds.

In short: practise sitting or lying down somewhere safe, never in or near water or while driving, never force a breath hold, and stop whenever your body tells you to.

How to prepare

  • Read the session page first. Each one sets out the rounds, pace and hold times so you know what is coming.
  • Practise on an empty or light stomach. Active breathing is more comfortable an hour or two after a meal.
  • Start gently. If you are new to breathwork, begin with a calm session before trying fast breathing or holds.
  • Set your own pace. The guidance is a suggestion. You can breathe more slowly, skip a round or pause the video at any time.

A suitable place to practise

Lie down or sit with support somewhere you could safely lose balance or awareness: a bed, a mat on the floor or a sofa. Loosen tight clothing and make sure you will not be disturbed.

Never practise in or near water (including baths, pools and the sea), while driving or operating machinery, while standing, or anywhere a fainting episode could injure you or someone else.

When not to practise

  • Under the influence of alcohol, recreational drugs or anything that affects your awareness.
  • When you are unwell, feverish or recovering from an illness, injury or surgery.
  • When you are very tired and need to stay alert afterwards, for example right before driving.
  • Our sessions are designed for adults. Children and teenagers should only practise with a responsible adult present, and should avoid fast breathing and breath holds.

Pregnancy, epilepsy and medical cautions

Do not practise fast breathing or breath holds if you are pregnant or have epilepsy or a history of seizures. Calm sessions with slow, gentle breathing may be suitable, but check with your midwife or doctor first.

Speak to a qualified healthcare professional before practising if you have, or are being treated for:

  • a heart or cardiovascular condition, including high blood pressure, arrhythmia or a history of stroke
  • a respiratory condition such as asthma or COPD
  • a serious or long-term medical condition, or you take medication that affects your heart, blood pressure or breathing
  • a mental health condition, including panic disorder, PTSD, psychosis or bipolar disorder, as intense breathwork can bring up strong emotions
  • eye conditions such as glaucoma or retinal problems, or recent surgery, where pressure from straining could be harmful

If you are unsure whether breathwork is right for you, choose a calm session and ask a professional before trying anything more active.

Tingling, dizziness and emotion

Fast, deep breathing lowers the carbon dioxide in your blood. This commonly causes tingling in the hands, feet or face, light-headedness, feeling warm or cold, and sometimes stiffness or cramping in the hands. These usually pass within a minute or two of returning to natural breathing. If they feel strong, slow down.

Breathwork can also bring up emotion: tears, laughter, restlessness or memories. This is a normal part of the practice for many people. Let it move through you, slow your breathing if it feels like too much, and stop if you feel overwhelmed. Take time to rest afterwards and reach out to someone you trust if you need support.

When to stop immediately

Stop the practice, return to natural breathing and rest if you notice:

  • chest pain, pressure or tightness
  • a racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat
  • feeling faint, confused or as if you might lose consciousness
  • a severe headache, blurred vision or numbness on one side of the body
  • breathlessness that does not settle, or panic that keeps building

If chest pain, a severe headache or other worrying symptoms do not settle quickly after you stop, seek medical help straight away (999 in the UK, 112 in the EU).

Breath-hold guidance

  • Hold times are guides, never targets. Breathe whenever your body asks for air, even if the timer is still running.
  • Never strain. Hold with a relaxed body, face and jaw. Do not bear down or push against a closed throat.
  • The urge to breathe can be delayed. After fast breathing you may not feel the need for air until late in a hold, and fainting can happen without warning. This is why lying down, and staying away from water, matters so much.
  • Inhale and exhale holds feel different. Holds after an exhale usually feel shorter and more demanding. Start with shorter versions and build up over weeks, not in one session.
  • Never compete. Do not compare your hold times with the timer, with others, or with your own past sessions.
  • Recover fully with a few natural breaths between rounds and at the end before you sit or stand up.
