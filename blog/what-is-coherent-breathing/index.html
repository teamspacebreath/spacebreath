What is coherent breathing?

Six seconds in, six seconds out, through the nose. What coherent breathing is, where it comes from, how to do it, and how we use it in our sessions.

Words by Harry and Jonas14 October 20268-minute readResearch sources listed below

Coherent breathing is a gentle practice built around one steady rhythm. You breathe in slowly through the nose, breathe out for the same length of time, and keep that rhythm going.

There are no breath holds and nothing to force: just a steady, even circle of breath at around five breaths a minute. In this guide we explain what coherent breathing is, where it comes from, how to do it and what the research says about it. We also share how it shapes our own sessions, along with a few words from Jonas, who guides them.

Coherent breathing, simply put

Coherent breathing is slow, paced breathing where the inhale and the exhale are the same length. Most versions sit at around five to six breaths a minute, so each inhale and each exhale lasts roughly five to six seconds.

In our sessions we use six seconds in and six seconds out, through the nose. One full breath takes 12 seconds, which works out at five breaths a minute. Most adults at rest breathe somewhere between 12 and 20 times a minute, so it's a noticeable change of pace.

You'll also see it called resonance breathing or resonant breathing. In research, the closely related idea is resonance frequency breathing: the pace at which heart rhythm and breathing fall most closely into step. For most adults that's somewhere around six breaths a minute.

How is it different from slow breathing or box breathing?

Slow breathing is a broad term, usually meaning anything under about ten breaths a minute. Coherent breathing is one specific form of it: a steady pace, an equal inhale and exhale, and no pauses.

Box breathing has four equal parts: breathe in, hold, breathe out, hold. The holds give it a clear structure, and many people like it for focus. Coherent breathing has no holds at all, only two phases flowing into each other, so it tends to feel softer and easier to keep up for longer. If you'd like to feel the difference, try our box breathing session alongside a coherent one.

Where coherent breathing comes from

Slowing the breath on purpose is nothing new. Yogic traditions have worked with it for centuries through pranayama. The name coherent breathing is generally credited to Stephen Elliott, an American engineer and author who developed and taught the method from the early 2000s, working at about five breaths a minute.

In parallel, researchers were reaching a similar rhythm from a different direction. From the 1990s, a team led by Paul Lehrer at Rutgers studied heart rate variability biofeedback and found that heart rate rises and falls with the breath most strongly at around six breaths a minute. Their review of how heart rate variability biofeedback works is a good starting point if you want the science in more depth. The term has since appeared in clinical research too: a Boston University study combining yoga with coherent breathing used a pace of five breaths a minute.

Many people, Jonas included, first met coherent breathing through James Nestor's book Breath: The New Science of a Lost Art (2020). Nestor describes a rhythm of about 5.5 seconds in and 5.5 seconds out, and the book brought slow, nasal breathing to a much wider audience.

How to do coherent breathing

  1. Find a comfortable position. Sit upright or lie down somewhere safe. Let your shoulders and jaw soften.
  2. Breathe through your nose. Keep the breath gentle and quiet, in and out.
  3. Breathe in for a slow count of six. Let the belly expand softly rather than lifting the chest.
  4. Breathe out for a count of six. Let the air flow out at the same pace, without pushing.
  5. Keep the circle going. Move from inhale to exhale with as little pause as feels comfortable.
  6. Start with about five minutes. Then return to your natural breathing and notice how you feel.

Counting in your head works, but it's easier with something to follow: a voice, a slow metronome, or music written to the rhythm. That's exactly why we make our sessions the way we do.

If six seconds feels too long

That's normal at first. Shorten the count to four or five seconds and let it lengthen over time. The count is a guide, not a target. The breath should stay soft and comfortable, never an effort. If you start to feel short of air or strained, shorten the count or return to natural breathing.

Why people practise coherent breathing

Most people come to coherent breathing because it helps them slow down. It's quiet, needs no equipment and fits into a few minutes almost anywhere: before work, between meetings, or at the end of the day.

What the research says

Much of the research centres on heart rate variability (HRV), the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Your heart speeds up slightly as you breathe in and slows as you breathe out. Breathing at around six breaths a minute makes those waves larger and more regular, which is why the practice is sometimes called HRV breathing.

  • A systematic review of slow breathing studies in healthy adults found that breathing below ten breaths a minute consistently increased heart rate variability, along with changes in brain activity and in how people felt.
  • In a small randomised study on resonance frequency breathing, people who breathed at their resonance pace for 15 minutes reported better mood afterwards than those who sat quietly. Their systolic blood pressure was also lower during a stressful task that followed.
  • A meta-analysis of 24 studies on heart rate variability biofeedback, an approach built around breathing at this pace, found reductions in self-reported stress and anxiety. The authors note that more well-controlled studies are needed.

These findings are encouraging, but many of the studies are small, and some involve guided biofeedback programmes rather than breathing alone. Coherent breathing isn't a treatment for any condition. We think of it as a gentle way to give yourself a few calmer minutes.

Four questions for Jonas

Jonas guides our English sessions. We asked him about coherent breathing.

In your own words, what is coherent breathing?

“It's breathing in for about six seconds and out for about six. In Breath, the rhythm is around five and a half to six seconds each way, and there are good reasons it keeps coming up in research. It's one of my favourite rhythms.”

What would you say to someone trying it for the first time?

“It's something you can practise any time during the day. It relaxes you and slows you down.”

What surprises people when they start?

“It feels quite slow in the beginning, because most people breathe pretty fast. You have to get used to it a bit, and that's fine.”

How did you first come across it, and what keeps you practising?

“I first read about coherent breathing in James Nestor's book Breath. What keeps me coming back is simple: it's relaxing and calming.”

Before you begin

Practise with care

Coherent breathing is gentle, but please practise with care.

  • Sit or lie down somewhere safe and comfortable. Do not practise while driving, in or near water, or anywhere a loss of consciousness could be dangerous.
  • If you feel uncomfortable, dizzy or unwell, stop, return to natural breathing and rest.
  • Do not practise if you are pregnant or have epilepsy, a cardiovascular condition or a serious respiratory condition.
  • This article is for education and general wellbeing and is not medical advice. Consult a qualified healthcare professional if you're unsure whether breathwork is suitable for you.

Frequently asked questions

Is coherent breathing the same as resonance breathing?

They're very closely related. Resonance breathing means breathing at the pace where your heart rhythm and breath fall most closely into step, which for most adults is around six breaths a minute. Coherent breathing uses a fixed pace of about five to six breaths a minute, so in everyday use the two terms are often interchangeable.

How many breaths per minute is coherent breathing?

Usually around five to six. Our sessions use six seconds in and six seconds out, which makes 12 seconds per breath, or five breaths a minute.

How long should I practise coherent breathing each day?

Five minutes is a good start. Many people build up to 10 or 20 minutes, and the research studies we mention used sessions of 15 to 20 minutes. Our English sessions run for 7 and 14 minutes.

Should I breathe through my nose or mouth?

We breathe through the nose for both the inhale and the exhale. Nasal breathing tends to be quieter and easier to slow down. If your nose is blocked, breathe gently through your mouth or practise another time.

What does HRV have to do with coherent breathing?

HRV, or heart rate variability, is the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Breathing at around five to six breaths a minute makes those variations larger and more regular. That's why coherent breathing is sometimes called HRV breathing.

Can I practise coherent breathing during the day?

Yes. That's part of its appeal. A few minutes at your desk, on a break or before bed is enough to try it, as long as you're somewhere safe and not driving.
