Creating “Release Thoughts & Tension”

How we built a 16-minute breathwork journey around release, and discovered that the music needed space of its own.

Words by Harry (SpaceBreath)15 September 20263-minute read

Every SpaceBreath session begins with an intention. For this one, we wanted to create a gentle space in which people could observe what they were carrying, soften the tension held in the body and gradually let it go.

Jonas began by writing and practising the breathwork over the course of a day. He explored how the pace of each breath changed the feeling of the session, gradually shaping three rounds that move from slow, conscious breathing into a soft rhythm and finish with a relaxed breath hold.

Once the structure was ready, he sent it over to me to begin composing the music.

Writing music that breathes with you

The music was created at 60 BPM, meaning every beat represents exactly one second. Different time signatures were then used to build the breathing pattern directly into the composition:

  • 10/4Ten slow breaths: four seconds in and six seconds out
  • 8/4Fifteen regular breaths: three seconds in and five seconds out
  • 4/4Transitions, breath holds, the introduction and the closing

Rather than simply playing behind the guidance, the music was designed to breathe alongside it.

I created a working template with markers showing exactly where each inhale, exhale and hold should happen. Jonas used this to record the guidance, which was then brought back into the Logic project so the voice, breath cues and music could be shaped together.

Finding the right feeling

The first version was more rhythmic, with heavier drums and more layers competing for attention. It worked musically, but it did not quite match the meditative experience Jonas had imagined. He wanted the breathing to feel softer and the music to leave more space.

I’m glad he said it. Looking back, I realised I had got carried away with the whole production.

After discussing it together and recording some softer breaths, I stripped back the drums and removed some of the busier elements. The bass and kick became a pulse, surrounded by deep pads, sub drones, hand pans, strings, vibraphone, singing bowls and gentle swells.

“But so calming, wow.”Jonas, after practising the first round

We both laughed, partly from relief and partly because the ideas had finally become the experience we had been searching for.

That is one of our favourite parts of creating SpaceBreath sessions. They rarely arrive fully formed. They develop through practice, listening, experimentation and honest conversations about what feels right. Sometimes we have to remove more than we add.

Eventually, the breathing, voice and music begin to feel like one journey.
